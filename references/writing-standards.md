# Writing Standards — the house style for everything this kit writes

Every piece of copy this kit produces — résumé bullets, the cover letter, application
answers, outreach notes, even the framing in a prep kit — should read like the user
wrote it themselves on a clear day. These are **advisory standards**, not a scoring
gate: hold to them while writing, so the output adheres by construction rather than
needing a separate cleanup pass. They sit alongside the two rules that always win —
**never fabricate** (every claim traces to `fact-pack.md` or something the user said)
and **write in the user's voice** (`voice.md` governs; where it conflicts with anything
here, the voice wins).

The goal isn't "less robotic." It's writing that carries a real person's judgment,
specificity, and rhythm. The patterns below are the structural fingerprints of
machine-written prose — name them so you can avoid them while drafting.

---

## The structural tells to avoid

These are *patterns*, not banned words. The same word is fine in one place and a tell in
another; what you're avoiding is the mechanical shape, not the vocabulary. For each:
what it is, why it reads as AI, and the human move instead.

1. **Anaphora / stacked parallelism** — consecutive sentences or fragments sharing the
   same opening structure. "Faster onboarding. Faster reviews. Faster shipping." It
   reads as manufactured cadence. *Instead:* vary how clauses open; let one strong
   sentence carry the point rather than three drumbeats.

2. **The false-negative setup** — defining something by what it isn't first. "It wasn't
   a redesign, it was a rethink." "This isn't about speed, it's about trust." One use
   can land; as a habit it's the single most common AI tell. *Instead:* state the thing
   directly. "I rethought the flow from scratch."

3. **Antithesis / reversal framing** — contrasting two ideas in tight parallel for
   drama. "Most teams chase output. The best teams protect focus." *Instead:* make the
   point without the symmetrical setup-and-turn; say what you found and why.

4. **Asyndeton** — list with the conjunctions stripped out for effect. "No handoffs, no
   wireframes, no waiting." *Instead:* write it as a normal sentence with "and," or as a
   real list if the context (a résumé) calls for one.

5. **Mechanism-contrast / abstract-superiority** — comparing two things with vague
   compounding language. "The gains accrue in ways a one-off fix never could."
   *Instead:* name the concrete mechanism and number if you have one.

6. **Rhetorical question as a transition** — a question asked only to answer it. "The
   result? A 40% lift." "Why does this matter?" *Instead:* just make the statement. "The
   result was a 40% lift."

7. **Clever comparative ending** — closing on a contrast for a mic-drop. "Average
   designers ship screens. Great ones ship decisions." *Instead:* end on the concrete
   outcome or a plain forward line; let the substance be the punch.

8. **Vague observational authority** — experience claims with no specifics. "I've seen
   this play out again and again." "Time and again, the pattern holds." *Instead:* cite
   the actual instance — "At Floma, this happened twice in six months."

9. **Staccato fragments for effect** — a sentence fragment standing in for a clause to
   sound punchy. "Built the whole thing. No PM. No handoff." *Instead:* let it flow —
   "I built the whole thing without a PM or a handoff." A genuinely short sentence is
   fine; a fragment deployed for drama is the tell.

10. **Empty intensifier openers** — "Here's the thing," "What if I told you," "Let that
    sink in," "Make no mistake." *Instead:* delete the runway and start at the point.

11. **Symmetry and triads on autopilot** — everything arriving in threes, every sentence
    the same length, a too-even rhythm. Real writing is lumpy. *Instead:* vary sentence
    length deliberately; break an expected triad to two or four.

## The positive half — what makes prose read as human

Avoiding the tells isn't enough; these are what you write *toward*:

- **Specificity over phrasing.** A concrete number, name, tool, or outcome does more
  than any rhetorical device. "Cut review cycles 40%" beats any clever framing of the
  same idea.
- **Natural conjunctions and connectors** — so, but, because, and, which meant. They're
  how people actually link thoughts; AI strips them for false economy.
- **Varied sentence length.** Let a short sentence land a point and a longer one carry
  context. Evenness is the tell; variation is the signal.
- **Logical build.** Each sentence should follow from the last — a real through-line,
  not a sequence of independently impressive lines.
- **Plain human punctuation** — a parenthetical aside, a dash where the user actually
  uses one (per `voice.md`), a comma splice they'd really write. Restraint on em dashes
  if the user overuses them or `voice.md` flags it.
- **One idea, fully.** Better to make a single point with real evidence than to gesture
  at three.

## Calibrate to the format

These are professional first-person documents, not punchy social copy, so the standard
flexes by context — this is the part a generic "avoid AI writing" rule gets wrong:

- **Résumé bullets** — terse, metric-led, fragment-normal (a résumé bullet *is* a
  fragment, and that's correct). The tells to police here are stacked parallel bullets
  that all open the same way and buzzword padding. Vary bullet openings; lead with the
  outcome.
- **Cover letter** — flowing first-person prose. This is where tells #2, #3, #6, #7, and
  #9 most often creep in. Hold the cover-letter playbook's craft rules; this standard
  reinforces them.
- **Application answers** — conversational but substantive. Answer the actual question
  first; avoid the rhetorical-question runway and the manufactured turn.
- **Outreach (email / LinkedIn)** — short and personal. The danger is sounding like cold
  AI outreach: lead with a genuine specific, keep the conjunctions, no asyndeton lists,
  no "Here's why I'm perfect for this."

A word the user genuinely uses ("most of my work was…") is not banned because "Most" can
start an AI sentence. The pattern is the target, not the vocabulary. **When in doubt,
`voice.md` decides** — match the real person over any rule here.

---

## How to apply this while writing

Don't write a draft and then hunt for violations. Hold these in mind *as* you draft, so
the first version already reads human. After drafting, one quick re-read with two
questions is enough: *Does any sentence exist only for cadence rather than content? Would
the user actually say it this way?* If yes to the first or no to the second, rewrite that
line to flow and to sound like them. And never manufacture edits for their own sake — if
a line is already clean and true and sounds like the user, leave it.
